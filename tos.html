Method: mid-price violations are not arbitrage

A no-arbitrage bound is a statement about tradeable prices. You buy at the ask and sell at the bid; you never trade at the mid. A bound that fails on mids but holds on the bid/ask you would actually pay is a quoting artifact, not a free lunch.

Every test below is therefore reported twice: once on mids (the textbook form, useful for spotting where the surface is ragged) and once on the executable bid/ask leg prices (the form that decides whether money is on the table). This distinction is the entire story of this chain — the mid tests fail in five places and the executable tests fail in none.

1. Intrinsic Value Floor
An option cannot be bought for less than its immediate exercise value.
CALLS: Ask ≥ max(0, S − K) = max(0, 13.89 − K)
PUTS: Ask ≥ max(0, K − S) = max(0, K − 13.89)

The executable form uses the ask, not the mid: to capture an intrinsic-value violation you must buy the option, and buying happens at the ask.

2. Strike Monotonicity
Calls must be non-increasing in strike; puts must be non-decreasing in strike.
CALLS: C(K₁) ≥ C(K₂) for K₁ < K₂
PUTS: P(K₁) ≤ P(K₂) for K₁ < K₂

The executable test for a put violation: sell the lower strike at its bid, buy the higher strike at its ask. If that package is a net credit you have a riskless profit, because the higher-strike put can never be worth less at expiry.

3. Vertical Spread Bound (Lipschitz Condition)
A call spread can never cost more than the distance between its strikes.
0 ≤ C(K₁) − C(K₂) ≤ K₂ − K₁

The spread's maximum payoff at expiry is K₂ − K₁, so paying more than that guarantees a loss — and receiving more than that when selling it is riskless profit. Executable form: the credit from selling the spread is bid(K₁) − ask(K₂), which must not exceed K₂ − K₁.

4. Butterfly Convexity
Option prices must be a convex function of strike.
V(K − Δ) − 2·V(K) + V(K + Δ) ≥ 0

A butterfly has a non-negative payoff in every state of the world, so it can never be entered for a credit. Executable form: buy both wings at the ask, sell two bodies at the bid; the net must be a debit.

5. Put–Call Parity
The single strongest constraint: two portfolios with identical payoffs must cost the same.
C + K·e−rT = P + S   ≈   C + K = P + 13.89

Long call plus cash equal to the strike, and long put plus long stock, both pay max(ST, K) at expiry. With roughly two weeks to expiry and no dividend, the discount factor moves the left side by about one cent at these strikes — smaller than a single tick, so the undiscounted form is used and the residual is noted below.

A parity gap is only tradeable if it exceeds the cost of crossing both spreads. The last column applies that filter.

A note on the sign of the gaps

The low strikes (8–11) show C + K slightly below P + S, while the high strikes (12–13) show the reverse. Discounting alone predicts the opposite for every strike: C + K·e−rT = P + S means C + K should sit above P + S by K(1 − e−rT), roughly one cent here. The observed pattern is instead the signature of American-style early-exercise value and bid-skew concentrated in the deep-ITM calls — all of it well inside the tradeable band.

Verdict: Parity holds executably at every strike with a trustworthy quote.

✓ What the chain gets right

  • Not one executable arbitrage across five independent tests
  • Put–call parity within 4 cents at every liquid strike
  • Strict monotonicity in both calls and puts
  • Uniform +0.06 ask cushion above intrinsic on ITM calls — consistent, systematic market making

⚠ Where the surface is ragged

  • OTM puts quoting 0.01/0.16 and 0.03/0.12 — spreads exceeding the option's own value
  • Every mid-price breach traced to those wide-spread strikes
  • Deep-ITM calls carry bid skew that drags mids under intrinsic
  • One transcription error ($14 put duplicating the $14 call) that manufactured the only apparent arbitrage
CONCLUSION: This chain contains no arbitrage. All eight apparent violations dissolve once the tests are run on the prices you could actually trade rather than on mid-quotes, and the single tradeable-looking gap is a data-entry artifact. The broader lesson is methodological: mid-price no-arbitrage tests generate false positives at exactly the strikes where liquidity is thinnest, which is where a naive screen will send you looking for free money. The correct screen prices every leg at the side you would have to cross.
