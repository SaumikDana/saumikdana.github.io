No-Arbitrage Conditions

The model-free constraints that bind every option chain — derived, ordered, and tested on live APLD quotes

What a No-Arbitrage Condition Is

Almost everything written about option pricing is a model: assume a diffusion, assume a volatility, assume a distribution, and out comes a number. No-arbitrage conditions are the opposite kind of statement. They assume nothing about how the stock moves. They are pure dominance arguments — if portfolio A pays at least as much as portfolio B in every state of the world at expiry, then A must cost at least as much as B today, or you have a money pump.

That makes these conditions the hard floor beneath every pricing model. Black–Scholes can be wrong about the volatility and still be a legitimate price. But an option chain that violates convexity is not "wrong about volatility" — it is internally incoherent, and no distribution, no model, no amount of vol-surface fitting can rescue it.

The generating principle

Build two portfolios out of options, stock and cash. Compare their payoffs at expiry across every possible terminal price ST. Whatever payoff relation holds there must hold between today's prices. Every condition on this page is that one move, applied to a different pair of portfolios.

Prices you can trade

A no-arbitrage condition is a claim about executable prices. You buy at the ask and sell at the bid; the mid is a display convention, not a price anyone transacts at. So each condition below has two forms:

The textbook form writes the condition on a single price per option. It describes the idealised, frictionless surface, and it is the right form for building intuition and for spotting where a chain is ragged.

The executable form prices every leg at the side you would have to cross. It is the form that decides whether money is actually on the table.

The gap between the two is not noise to be apologised for — it is the measurement. As shown at the end, the textbook form fails in eight places on this chain and the executable form fails in none, and where it fails maps precisely onto where liquidity is thinnest. The mid-price surface is a liquidity map wearing the costume of a pricing anomaly.

A Ladder of Conditions

The conditions are not a checklist of unrelated rules. They form a hierarchy organised by how many derivatives of the call price with respect to strike they constrain, and — as the closing section shows — they are collectively equivalent to a single statement. Read in this order they build on each other:

Note the asymmetry. The first four are inequalities — one-sided constraints that leave a range of admissible prices. The last two are equalities, and are correspondingly the sharpest tests: there is exactly one right answer, and any deviation beyond transaction costs is a trade.

Order 0 — Intrinsic Floor and Ceiling
An option is worth at least what exercising it right now would yield, and never more than the thing it controls.
max(0, S − K) ≤ C(K) ≤ S
max(0, K − S) ≤ P(K) ≤ K·e−rT

The argument. A call gives the right to buy at K. If it traded below S − K you would buy the call, exercise, sell the stock, and bank the difference with no capital at risk and no exposure to what happens next. That is the floor. The ceiling is equally direct: the call confers strictly less than owning the stock outright — it caps your downside but costs you the right to the shares unconditionally — so it cannot cost more than the stock.

Executable form. Capturing a floor violation requires buying the option, so the test runs on the ask, not the mid. This distinction matters most exactly where it is most tempting to skip: deep in-the-money strikes, where the option is nearly all intrinsic and the spread is a large fraction of the time value.

What the low mid actually encodes is bid skew. Notice that the ask cushion is exactly +0.06 at the $8, $9 and $10 strikes. That uniformity is the signature of a systematic quoting rule, not a pricing error — a genuine mispricing would not be so tidy.
Result: Floor and ceiling hold at every strike on an executable basis. One mid-price breach ($8 call), zero executable breaches.
Order 1 — Monotonicity and the Lipschitz Bound
The slope of the call price in strike must lie between −1 and 0. Both ends of that interval are a separate arbitrage.
0 ≤ C(K₁) − C(K₂) ≤ K₂ − K₁   for   K₁ < K₂
0 ≤ P(K₂) − P(K₁) ≤ K₂ − K₁

The argument. Consider the call vertical: long K₁, short K₂. At expiry it pays 0 below K₁, rises to K₂ − K₁ at K₂, and stays flat above. That payoff is never negative, so the spread cannot be entered for a credit — the left inequality, monotonicity. It also never exceeds K₂ − K₁, so the spread cannot cost more than the strike width — the right inequality, the Lipschitz bound.

These are genuinely two different conditions and a chain can satisfy one while breaching the other. Monotonicity says the curve slopes downward; the Lipschitz bound says it cannot slope downward faster than 45°. A chain checked only for ordering will sail past a Lipschitz breach without noticing.

Order 2 — Convexity
Option prices must be a convex function of strike. This is the deepest of the inequalities and the one with a probabilistic meaning.
V(K − Δ) − 2·V(K) + V(K + Δ) ≥ 0

The argument. That expression is the cost of a butterfly: long one wing at each outer strike, short two bodies at the centre. Its payoff is a tent — zero outside the wings, peaking at Δ at the centre — and it is never negative anywhere. A portfolio that can never pay out less than zero cannot be entered for a credit.

Why this one matters more than the others. Scaled by Δ², the butterfly cost is the discounted risk-neutral probability that the stock lands near the centre strike. Convexity is therefore not merely a pricing constraint — it is the requirement that the probability density implied by the chain be non-negative. A chain that breaches convexity is asserting a negative probability. No volatility number can fix that, because the incoherence is not in the vol, it is in the measure.

Executable form. Buy both wings at the ask, sell two bodies at the bid. The net must be a debit.

The First Equality — Put–Call Parity
Not an inequality but an identity. The strongest constraint on the chain, and the only one that ties options to the underlying.
C(K) + K·e−rT = P(K) + S

The argument. Hold a call plus cash equal to the present value of the strike. At expiry, if ST > K you exercise and own the stock; otherwise you hold K in cash. The payoff is max(ST, K). Now hold a put plus the stock. If ST < K you exercise and receive K; otherwise you hold the stock. The payoff is again max(ST, K). Identical payoffs in every state, therefore identical cost today.

What makes parity uniquely sharp is that it is two-sided. The inequalities above leave an admissible range; parity leaves a single number. Any deviation beyond the cost of crossing the spreads is a trade in one direction or the other, and it is the condition most aggressively policed by market makers for exactly that reason.

With roughly two weeks to expiry and no dividend, the discount factor shifts the left side by about a cent at these strikes — below one tick — so the undiscounted form is used and the residual is addressed below. A gap is tradeable only if it exceeds the cost of crossing both spreads; the final column applies that filter.

Reading the sign of the residuals

The gaps are not randomly signed. Strikes $8–$11 show C + K slightly below P + S; strikes $12–$13 show the reverse. Discounting alone predicts the opposite everywhere: since C + K·e−rT = P + S, the undiscounted C + K should sit above P + S by K(1 − e−rT), about a cent here.

The observed pattern instead tracks moneyness. The negative residuals are concentrated in the deep-ITM calls, where American early-exercise value and the bid skew described in Order 0 both push the call's effective price down relative to European parity. The crossover to positive residuals happens right where the calls stop being deep ITM — between $11 and $12, with spot at $13.89. That is a structural signature, not scatter, and every value of it sits well inside the tradeable band.

Result: Parity holds at every strike, with all residuals inside the bid–ask band.
The Second Equality — The Box Spread
Four options combining into a synthetic zero-coupon bond. The condition that removes the underlying from the picture entirely.
[C(K₁) − C(K₂)] + [P(K₂) − P(K₁)] = (K₂ − K₁)·e−rT

The argument. A call vertical plus a put vertical at the same two strikes pays K₂ − K₁ in every state of the world. Below K₁ the puts deliver it; above K₂ the calls do; in between the two legs hand off continuously. The position has no exposure to the stock at all — it is a riskless bond assembled from four options, and it must therefore cost the discounted face value.

This is the purest of the conditions. Parity still references S, so a stale underlying quote contaminates it. The box references nothing but the four option prices. Whatever it prices at is the market's option-implied financing rate, which is why boxes are traded in size as a cash-management instrument rather than as a directional view.

One Condition, Not Six

The ladder above is easier to remember once you see that its rungs are not independent. Differentiate the call price with respect to strike and something remarkable falls out. For a European call,

C(K) = e−rT ∫ max(ST − K, 0) · q(ST) dST

and therefore

−∂C/∂K = e−rT · Q(ST > K)      ∂²C/∂K² = e−rT · q(K)

The first derivative of the call curve is the risk-neutral survival function. The second derivative is the risk-neutral density. Every inequality on this page is now readable as a requirement on that distribution:

ConditionEquivalent probabilistic statement
Monotonicity: ∂C/∂K ≤ 0Probabilities are non-negative: Q(ST > K) ≥ 0
Lipschitz: |∂C/∂K| ≤ 1Probabilities do not exceed one: Q(ST > K) ≤ 1
Convexity: ∂²C/∂K² ≥ 0The density is non-negative: q(K) ≥ 0
Intrinsic floor / ceilingThe distribution has the right support and total mass
Put–call parityCalls and puts are priced under the same measure
Box spreadThat measure discounts at a single consistent rate

This is the fundamental theorem of asset pricing in miniature: a chain is arbitrage-free precisely when there exists some probability distribution consistent with all of its prices at once. The six conditions are six necessary features of that distribution, not six unrelated rules. Passing all of them is the statement that such a measure exists; failing any one is the statement that no distribution — not the one your model produces, not any other — can reproduce these prices.

That single sentence is the whole result, and it says something sharper than a violation count: mids are not a slightly noisy version of the price surface, they are a construct with no guarantee of internal consistency at all.

Conditions Beyond a Single Expiry

Everything above is static — one expiry, one snapshot. Two further families of condition bind a full surface and cannot be tested from this chain alone.

Calendar monotonicity

For the same strike, a longer-dated American option cannot be worth less than a shorter-dated one — it confers every right the shorter option does, plus more time. For European options the statement holds on forward-adjusted strikes. In total-variance terms, w(K, T) = σ²impT must be non-decreasing in T; a surface where it dips implies negative forward variance.

American early exercise

These are American options, so the European bounds are floors rather than identities. An American call on a non-dividend payer is never optimally exercised early and matches its European value; an American put can be, so PAmer ≥ PEuro, and put–call parity relaxes into a two-sided inequality: S − K ≤ C − P ≤ S − K·e−rT. The negative parity residuals at the deep-ITM strikes sit inside exactly this band.

CONCLUSION: This chain is arbitrage-free. Every one of the six static conditions holds on the prices you could actually trade, and put–call parity — the sharpest of them, the one with no admissible range at all — holds tightest of any. The eight breaches visible on mid prices are not near-misses on those bounds; they are artifacts of a quantity that was never guaranteed to be a price. Every one sits in a strike where the spread rivals or exceeds the option's own value, and together they imply a probability distribution that cannot exist. The practical lesson is that a no-arbitrage screen run on mids will point you hardest at precisely the strikes where you can least afford to trade — it finds illiquidity and reports it as opportunity. Price every leg at the side you must cross, and the free lunches disappear, which is exactly what the theory predicts.
