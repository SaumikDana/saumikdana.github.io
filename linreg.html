The Two-Level Reality of Regression

Understanding regression requires distinguishing between what exists in reality versus what we can observe and estimate from our sample data.

Population Level (True but Unknown)

Simple linear regression assumes there's a true relationship in the population:

$$ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1x + \epsilon $$

Where:

  • $\beta_0$ = true population intercept (unknown parameter)
  • $\beta_1$ = true population slope (unknown parameter)
  • $\epsilon$ = random error term with mean 0

These Greek letter parameters represent reality—the actual relationship that would exist if we could observe the entire population.

$$\beta_1 = \rho_{XY} \frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}$$
$$\beta_0 = \mu_Y - \beta_1 \mu_X$$

Where:

  • $\rho_{XY} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}$ is the population correlation coefficient
  • $\sigma_X = \sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}$ and $\sigma_Y = \sqrt{\text{Var}(Y)}$ are population standard deviations
  • $\mu_X = E[X]$ and $\mu_Y = E[Y]$ are the population means

Sample Level (What We Estimate)

From our sample data, we estimate the population parameters using least squares:

$$ \hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1x $$

Where:

  • $\hat{\beta}_0$ = sample estimate of the true intercept
  • $\hat{\beta}_1$ = sample estimate of the true slope
  • $\hat{y}$ = predicted value of $y$ for a given $x$

The "hat" notation ($\hat{}$) always means "estimate of" or "predicted."

$$ \hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2} $$
$$ \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1\bar{x} $$

Important distinction:

  • $\epsilon$: The true, unobservable error in the population model
  • $e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i$: The observable residuals from our fitted model

Residuals are our best approximation of the true errors, but they're not the same thing.

Why We Care About the Distinction

This two-level framework matters because:

  • Inference: We want to make statements about $\beta_1$ (the true slope), not just $\hat{\beta}_1$ (our estimate)
  • Uncertainty: Our estimate $\hat{\beta}_1$ has sampling variability—different samples give different estimates
  • Hypothesis Testing: We test claims about the true parameter $\beta_1$, using our estimate $\hat{\beta}_1$

Standard Error of the Slope Estimate

$$ SE(\hat{\beta}_1) = \sqrt{\frac{\frac{1}{n-2} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}} $$

Sums of Squares and $R^2$

The error sum of squares (unexplained variation):

$$ SSE = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 $$

The total sum of squares (total variation in $y$):

$$ SST = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2 $$

The coefficient of determination, i.e. the fraction of variance explained:

$$ R^2 = 1 - \frac{SSE}{SST} = \frac{SST - SSE}{SST} = \frac{SSR}{SST} $$

If $SSE = 0 \Rightarrow R^2 = 1$ (perfect fit). If $SSE \approx SST \Rightarrow R^2 \approx 0$ (no explanatory power). If $SSE > SST \Rightarrow R^2 < 0$ (worse than predicting the mean).

Note that $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{SSE}{n-2}$, so the standard error above can be written as $SE(\hat{\beta}_1) = \sqrt{\hat{\sigma}^2 / \sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2}$.

t-Statistic for Testing $H_0: \beta_1 = 0$

$$ t = \frac{\hat{\beta}_1}{SE(\hat{\beta}_1)} = \frac{\hat{\beta}_1}{\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\sigma}^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2}}} $$

This t-statistic follows a t-distribution with $(n-2)$ degrees of freedom under the null hypothesis.

F-Statistic for Testing Overall Significance

For the simple model fitted here—one predictor, so one slope being tested:

$$ F = \frac{SSR/1}{SSE/(n-2)} = \frac{SST - SSE}{SSE/(n-2)} = t^2 $$

It follows an $F$-distribution with $(1, \, n-2)$ degrees of freedom under the null hypothesis. With one predictor the F-test and the t-test above are the same test written two ways; the F-test earns its keep once there are several predictors, which is where the next section picks up.

Doubling the Data Points

On $R^2$:

  • Adding data doesn't automatically change $R^2$—it depends on whether new points follow the same pattern
  • If new points are consistent with the existing relationship, $R^2$ may stay similar or slightly improve
  • If new points are more scattered, $R^2$ could decrease

On p-values:

  • More data typically reduces $SE(\hat{\beta}_1)$ (denominator gets larger due to more spread in $x$-values)
  • Smaller standard error means larger $|t|$-statistic for the same slope estimate
  • Larger $|t|$-statistic means smaller p-value, stronger evidence against $H_0: \beta_1 = 0$

On the analytical solutions for $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$:

  • Doubling the number of data points has no effect on the true population parameters themselves
  • $\beta_1 = \frac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)}{\text{Var}(X)}$ remains constant—it's a fixed property of the population relationship
  • $\beta_0 = \mu_Y - \beta_1 \mu_X$ also remains constant—it depends only on population moments
  • The correlation $\rho_{XY}$ and population standard deviations $\sigma_X, \sigma_Y$ are unchanged

On the least squares estimates:

  • The actual values of $\hat{\beta}_0$ and $\hat{\beta}_1$ will change (new sample, new estimates)

Key Takeaway: More data gives us better estimates of the same underlying truth, not a different truth.

Reading the F-statistic

A note on notation before we start. Everything above is simple regression: one predictor $x$, one intercept $\beta_0$, one slope $\beta_1$. The F-test is most naturally stated for the general case of $p$ predictors $x_1, \ldots, x_p$, where the model is

$$ y = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 x_1 + \gamma_2 x_2 + \cdots + \gamma_p x_p + \epsilon $$

The $\gamma_j$ here are different objects from the $\beta_0, \beta_1$ above—one slope attached to each of $p$ separate predictor columns, not a re-labelling of the two-parameter simple model. Simple regression is the special case $p = 1$, where $\gamma_0 = \beta_0$ and $\gamma_1 = \beta_1$. We use $\gamma$ purely to keep the two settings visually distinct.

1. The hypothesis it tests

The F-statistic asks one question about the model as a whole: do the predictors, taken together, do anything at all? Formally it tests

$$ H_0: \gamma_1 = \gamma_2 = \cdots = \gamma_p = 0 \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad H_A: \text{at least one } \gamma_j \neq 0 $$

Note that the intercept $\gamma_0$ is deliberately left out of the null—it is not being tested, it is the thing the model falls back to. Under $H_0$ every slope is zero at once, so the fitted equation collapses to $\hat{y} = \hat{\gamma}_0 = \bar{y}$. In other words, the null model is "just predict the mean of $y$ for everybody, and ignore the predictors entirely." The F-test compares your regression against that baseline.

2. Why it is a signal-to-noise ratio

$$ F = \frac{MSR}{MSE} = \frac{SSR/p}{SSE/(n-p-1)} = \frac{\text{explained variance per predictor}}{\text{unexplained variance per residual degree of freedom}} $$

Both the numerator and the denominator are variance estimates measured in the same units. The denominator, $MSE = SSE/(n-p-1)$, is an unbiased estimate of the true error variance $\sigma^2$ whether or not $H_0$ is true. The numerator, $MSR = SSR/p$, is also an unbiased estimate of $\sigma^2$ when $H_0$ is true—because in that case the only thing the predictors are picking up is random noise. Two estimates of the same quantity divided by each other give a ratio near 1.

That is the whole intuition: $F \approx 1$ is what "nothing is going on" looks like. When a real relationship exists, the numerator inflates by the systematic variation the predictors capture while the denominator stays anchored at $\sigma^2$, so $F$ climbs above 1.

3. What a given value tells you

Value of $F$ What it means Typical verdict
$F < 1$ The predictors explain less than random noise would be expected to explain by chance Fail to reject $H_0$; the model is no help
$F \approx 1$ Explained and unexplained variance are the same size per degree of freedom—exactly the null pattern Fail to reject $H_0$
$F$ moderately above 1 The predictors are pulling some real signal out of the noise Check the p-value—significance depends on the number of predictors and on $n$
$F \gg 1$ Explained variance dwarfs the residual noise Reject $H_0$; p-value will be very small

There is no universal cutoff. "Is $F = 4$ big?" has no answer without the degrees of freedom. With $(1, 5)$ df, $F = 4$ is unremarkable; with $(1, 500)$ df it is decisive. The raw $F$ is only meaningful once you convert it to a p-value against the $F_{p, \, n-p-1}$ distribution, which is why software always reports both.

4. What it does not tell you

  • It is a joint test, not a per-predictor test. A significant $F$ says at least one slope is nonzero; it does not say which. For that you need the individual t-tests.
  • Significance is not the same as usefulness. With a large enough $n$, a trivially small $R^2$ can still produce a significant $F$. Read $F$ for "is there a signal" and $R^2$ for "how much."
  • The two tests can disagree in multiple regression. Correlated predictors can give a significant $F$ while every individual t-test comes back insignificant—the predictors jointly explain $y$, but no single one is identifiable on its own.
  • In simple regression it adds nothing new. With $p = 1$, $F = t^2$ exactly, so the F-test and the t-test on $\beta_1$ are the same test written two ways.
